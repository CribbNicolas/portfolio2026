# Personal branding and self-positioning for an individual web developer (subtle, "pull" not "push")

Scope note for the report writer: this pass ran ~20 searches/fetches. Peer-reviewed evidence was found for
signaling, humblebragging, the pratfall effect, mere exposure, curiosity gaps, celebrity authenticity, and
cross-cultural self-promotion. Positioning frameworks (Dunford, Morgan, McKenzie, StoryBrand, Kleon, swyx) are
**practitioner opinion**, not tested research, and are labelled as such. Several requested items (Cialdini
specifics, Lee Robinson, Cassie Evans, interviews with Rauno/Emil, LatAm-specific data) could not be sourced
and are listed under Gaps rather than filled from memory.

## 1. Positioning frameworks for a single person (Dunford, category of one, niche vs generalist)

### Takeaway
Practitioners agree on one move: describe yourself by the outcome you produce for someone specific, not by
your job title or stack. "Pick a niche" is the standard freelance advice, but it is practitioner consensus with
known trade-offs, not an evidence-backed rule. No source applies Dunford's framework to job seekers directly.

### Cited Findings
- April Dunford's positioning work (books *Obviously Awesome* and *Sales Pitch*) is company/product-focused. A podcast summary puts its core question as "why pick you over the other guys", not "what's so great about you". The search found no source applying her method to individual job seekers — [Search summary of Dunford interviews: leanmarketing.com](https://leanmarketing.com/podcast/positioning-with-april-dunford); [Ungated Marketing interview](https://webflow.landbot.io/blog/personal-brand-positioning-april-dunford)
- In that interview Dunford says positioning *herself* as "the positioning expert" took a few years. She had to work out her consulting offerings, pricing and target clients. In other words, her own personal positioning was iterative, not decided upfront — [Ungated Marketing interview](https://webflow.landbot.io/blog/personal-brand-positioning-april-dunford)
- Patrick McKenzie, "Don't Call Yourself A Programmer" (2011, a classic practitioner essay): "Engineers are hired to create business value, not to program things." "Add revenue. Reduce costs." Describe yourself by "what you have accomplished for previous employers". "Anyone describing themselves as either a Java programmer or .NET programmer has already lost." "You are not defined by your chosen software stack." "Modesty is not a career-enhancing character trait." The essay does not discuss choosing a niche — [kalzumeus.com](https://www.kalzumeus.com/2011/10/28/dont-call-yourself-a-programmer/)
- Philip Morgan (positioning consultant for technical firms) argues that specializing lets a firm charge more, and gives a step-by-step method for picking and testing a niche. Quoted line: "You need to pick expensive problems if you want to pick a profitable niche" — [Business of Freelancing podcast](https://freelancing.transistor.fm/episodes/positioning-101-for-freelancers-w-philip-morgan); [WP-Tonic show notes](https://wp-tonic-show-a-wordpress-podcast.castos.com/episodes/163-philip-morgan-on-positioning-your-web-agency)
- Double Your Freelancing says specializing makes it easier to charge more and close deals, but that picking a niche "can be downright scary" — [doubleyourfreelancing.com, Branding and positioning](https://doubleyourfreelancing.com/category/branding-and-positioning/)
- Counterpoint: Jonathan Stark favours specializing, but he names a trade-off of vertical (industry) niches: clients may worry you will share their secrets with competitors — [jonathanstark.com, "Problems with specializing on a vertical market"](https://jonathanstark.com/daily/20170704-problems-with-specializing-on-a-vertical-market)

### Inferences
- For one person who wants both a job and freelance clients, the McKenzie framing (outcomes over title or stack) works for both audiences. A hard vertical niche (Morgan) mainly helps the freelance side and can narrow the job side. A practical compromise is a "problem niche" rather than an industry niche. For this developer that could be interactive maps or geospatial front-end, given the RPPL/Mapbox metrics already in the dataset. That problem can be stated on both surfaces.
- Dunford's "why you over the alternatives" question maps onto a hiring manager's real alternatives: another mid-level React dev, an agency, or doing nothing. A personal site can answer it implicitly through a concrete differentiator instead of an adjective.
- "Category of one" and category design (Play Bigger) were not sourced in this pass. Treat them as untested practitioner ideas.

### Gaps
- No primary source found that applies *Obviously Awesome*'s five components (competitive alternatives, unique attributes, value, target customers, market category) to an individual.
- No empirical study found comparing hiring or rates for specialist and generalist developers. The niche argument rests on practitioner experience.
- Not sourced: Play Bigger / category design, and "category of one" (Joe Calloway).

## 2. "Show, don't tell" and costly signaling

### Takeaway
Signaling theory, a classic from economics (Spence, 1973), has a clear conclusion: a credible signal is one that is cheaper for a high-quality sender to produce than for a low-quality one. Claims cost nothing to make, so they carry little information. Recent field experiments on hiring platforms show costly signals change employer behaviour. Extending this to personal branding is plausible, but it is a theoretical extension, not a tested finding.

### Cited Findings
- Signals are most credible when they are differentially costly: harder or more expensive for low-quality senders to produce or imitate. Spence's 1973 job-market model treats education as such a signal — [Wikipedia: Signalling (economics)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signalling_(economics))
- MIT IDE describes hiring-platform experiments with costly signals. Renting a badge raised the chance of an employer invite by about 4.8% per impression. Two years later, after the badge had gone platform-wide, badge renters still got about 50% more employer invites. The authors argue that "a signal that anyone can claim at any time for free will be claimed by everyone and mean nothing", and that AI has made applications cheaper and more alike — [MIT IDE, "Can costly signals fix hiring?"](https://ide.mit.edu/insights/can-costly-signals-fix-hiring/)
- A 2026 Frontiers in Psychology article proposes a signaling pathway from perception to employability. The authors themselves say their framework captures only part of the original theory — [Frontiers in Psychology 2026](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1726553/full)
- Countersignalling (high-quality senders sometimes *avoid* obvious signals to separate themselves from mid-quality ones) is a related concept, from a concept-explainer source — [conceptually.org](https://conceptually.org/concepts/signalling-and-countersignalling)
- Craft as a signal, practitioner examples: Bruno Simon's drivable 3D-car portfolio (2019, Three.js) launched with a single tweet. Reported reach was ~400,000 visits, and it won Awwwards Site of the Year 2020 (sources differ on the award level). Simon said in an interview that it "ended up" boosting his career significantly — [Creative Bloq](https://www.creativebloq.com/news/3d-car-portfolio); [Pastel](https://usepastel.com/blog/how-a-design-portfolio-got-the-attention-of-400-000-visitors); [Mux interview, 2024](https://www.mux.com/blog/3d-web-development-and-beyond-a-chat-with-bruno-simon)
- Rauno Freiberg (Staff Design Engineer at Vercel, formerly The Browser Company) has a portfolio that third-party readers praise for craft and detail and note he built himself. Third-party, enthusiast source — [Guo Chen Substack](https://guochen.substack.com/p/who-are-my-design-heroes)

### Inferences
- On a portfolio, the costly or hard-to-fake signals are things a generic template or an LLM cannot produce cheaply. Examples: a working, performant interactive artifact; measured numbers that can be traced back; visible engineering constraints (zero-JS CV, byte budgets, CI gates); and public source code. Adjectives ("rápido", "robusto", "atención al detalle") are free to claim, so they are weak signals.
- The project's existing setup already holds unusually strong signals: a 3D knowledge map with a no-JS SVG fallback, a PDF generated from data, and CI gates for accessibility and size. Stating *one* of these as a fact a reader can verify is worth more than describing the developer's qualities.
- Countersignalling suggests that not shouting (no "hire me!!", no skill-percentage bars) can itself read as a high-quality signal. This is a theoretical inference, not tested for portfolios.

### Gaps
- No empirical study found that measures recruiter or client response to "demonstrated" and "claimed" skills on developer portfolios specifically.
- No confirmed hiring outcome found for Bruno Simon's portfolio beyond his own "boosted my career" statement.

## 3. Storytelling for personal presentation (StoryBrand, hero's journey, problem→approach→outcome, scroll narrative)

### Takeaway
StoryBrand (Donald Miller) is the most-cited practitioner framework. Its central move is to make the *client* the hero and the professional the guide. This is practitioner doctrine, untested in peer-reviewed research, and has critics. For a scroll-based personal site, a reasonable adaptation is: the reader's problem → the guide's evidence → a plan → a low-friction call to action.

### Cited Findings
- StoryBrand's seven parts (SB7): (1) a character who wants something (the customer); (2) a problem, external and internal; (3) meets a guide (the brand); (4) who gives them a plan; (5) and calls them to action; (6) that helps them avoid failure; (7) and ends in success — [Gravity Global guide](https://www.gravityglobal.com/blog/complete-guide-storybrand-framework); [Big Red Jelly SB7 explainer](https://bigredjelly.com/blog/what-is-storybrand-and-how-do-you-build-a-brand-story-for-your-business/)
- Impact Plus calls StoryBrand a simplified hero's journey and orders the last two steps differently (success before failure) — [impactplus.com](https://www.impactplus.com/blog/what-is-the-storybrand-framework)
- A critical analysis (Yu-kai Chou) describes the framework as a "status inversion" and points to sharp critiques — [yukaichou.com](https://yukaichou.com/?p=52664)
- Austin Kleon, *Show Your Work!* (2014, practitioner). Principles include "Tell good stories", "Teach what you know", "Share something small every day", "Open up your cabinet of curiosities" and "Don't turn into human spam". He argues that sharing the process matters, not only polished final work — [publisher listing via imusic.no](https://www.imusic.no/books/9780761178972/austin-kleon-2014-show-your-work-10-ways-to-share-your-creativity-and-get-discovered-pocketbok); [booksthatslay summary](https://booksthatslay.com/show-your-work-austin-kleon-summary/)
- Curiosity as a pacing tool: Loewenstein (1994, *Psychological Bulletin* 116(1):75–98) treats curiosity as a deprivation caused by noticing a gap in knowledge. Secondary sources add that some prior knowledge is needed (total ignorance does not create curiosity) and that the gap must look closeable. The "moderate gap" claim is a popular-press reading, not verified against the paper — [stafforini.com summary of Loewenstein 1994](https://stafforini.com/works/loewenstein-1994-psychology-curiosity-review/); [PBS NOVA](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/nova/article/how-to-stimulate-curiosity/)

### Inferences
- A personal site serves two "heroes": the employer (hero = the team that needs to ship X) and the freelance client (hero = the business with problem Y). StoryBrand suggests the hero section should name *their* problem in their words. An "I'm Nicolas, I love code" opening fails that test.
- A scroll arc that matches both the frameworks and this site's structure:
  1. **Hook / open loop** (hero): one specific, slightly surprising claim or artifact that creates a closeable curiosity gap. Here, the interactive map itself.
  2. **Rising action** (map, then projects): problem → approach → measurable outcome, one project at a time, with constraints shown.
  3. **Climax**: the strongest single proof. The most quantified or most technically demanding case, or the live artifact the reader can try.
  4. **Resolution / CTA** (CV + contact): low pressure ("si tenés un problema parecido, escribime"), with the CV as proof and not as the pitch.
- The pratfall finding (section 5) suggests a short, honest "what I'd do differently" note inside a case study can work as the narrative "obstacle" without weakening competence. It only works once competence is established earlier in the scroll.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed test of StoryBrand's effectiveness found. Only practitioner explainers and one critique.
- No research found on narrative structure specifically for scroll-based personal or portfolio pages.

## 4. Authenticity and voice (specificity, humility vs confidence, humor)

### Takeaway
The evidence points to the same rule this project already uses. Perceived authenticity of a "human brand" comes from consistency over time and from distinctiveness (rarity), not from claiming to be authentic. Disguised bragging (humblebragging) reliably backfires. Plain, specific statements of accomplishment do better than false modesty.

### Cited Findings
- Moulard, Garrity & Rice (2015), *Psychology & Marketing* 32(2):173–186. Celebrity (human-brand) authenticity is "the perception that a celebrity behaves according to his or her true self". In a survey of 428 adults, **rarity** (being distinctive or uncommon) and **stability** (consistency over time) both raised perceived authenticity, and age moderated the effects — [LSU repository](https://repository.lsu.edu/marketing_pubs/49)
- Kucharska et al. (footballers on social media, n=496 Polish users): **consistency and candidness** were the strongest drivers of perceived authenticity — [Gdańsk Tech repository](https://pub.pg.edu.pl/pl/download/145864-personal-brand-authenticity-and-social-media-the-top-5-football-players-case)
- Sezer, Gino & Norton (2018), "Humblebragging: A distinct—and ineffective—self-presentation strategy", *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*. Humblebragging (bragging disguised as a complaint or as modesty) is common. It lowers liking and perceived sincerity, and it fails to signal the trait it aims at. Versions of the paper report between five and nine studies; the published one is authoritative — [Wharton-hosted PDF](https://marketing.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2017/12/Sezer_Misguided-Self-Presentation.pdf); [JPSP via Ovid](https://www.ovid.com/journals/jpspy/pdf/10.1037/pspi0000108~humblebragging-a-distinctand-ineffectiveself-presentation)
- HBS Working Knowledge's coverage applies the finding to job interviews. A straight brag outperformed a humblebrag, for example on the "biggest weakness" question — [HBS Working Knowledge](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/humblebragging-is-a-bad-strategy-especially-in-a-job-interview); [Pacific Standard](https://psmag.com/news/your-humblebrag-raises-a-red-flag/)
- Schmid Mast et al. (2011): self-promotion raised hireability *because* it raised perceived competence (see section 6 for the cultural moderation) — [UNIGE open archive](https://archive-ouverte.unige.ch/unige:101515)

### Inferences
- The project's test ("could someone else with my stack have written this exact sentence?") is in effect a test for **rarity** in the Moulard sense. A sentence anyone could write adds nothing distinctive, so it cannot add to perceived authenticity. That gives the rule a research footing beyond taste.
- **Stability** suggests the voice should stay consistent across the site, CV, LinkedIn and over time. The single-source-of-truth data model already enforces this structurally.
- Confidence and humility resolve as: state accomplishments plainly and specifically (a number, a constraint, a result), and admit limits plainly (e.g., "inglés A2"). Avoid the hybrid ("I'm just a humble dev who happened to…"), which is the humblebrag pattern.
- Humor: no study found. As a working rule, dry, specific humor tied to the work (a footnote, an easter egg in the map) is consistent with "candidness". Generic jokes are not.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study found on humor in professional self-presentation or portfolios.
- The authenticity studies cover celebrities and athletes, not developers or B2B professionals. Transfer is inferred.

## 5. Modern, non-salesy persuasion (Cialdini subtly, pratfall, curiosity gaps, reciprocity through giving value, mere exposure)

### Takeaway
The effects with the strongest evidence for "pull" are mere exposure (repeated, low-key presence raises liking, up to a point) and the pratfall effect (a small flaw helps *only* someone already seen as competent). Giving value publicly (writing, open source, teaching) is the main practitioner route to both exposure and reciprocity. Its value as a career strategy is backed by practitioner testimony, not controlled studies.

### Cited Findings
- **Pratfall effect.** Aronson, Willerman & Floyd (1966), *Psychonomic Science*, ~48 male University of Minnesota students. A highly competent candidate (92% correct on a quiz) became *more* likeable after spilling coffee. An average candidate (30% correct) became *less* likeable. Later work (Helmreich, Aronson & LeFan, 1970, "To err is humanizing — sometimes") found the effect depends on the observer's self-esteem. Gender and severity of the blunder also moderate it. No modern large-sample or pre-registered replication was found — [Wikipedia: Pratfall effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pratfall_effect); [Simon Whatley summary](https://www.simonwhatley.co.uk/writing/the-pratfall-effect/)
- A critical reading of the study: imperfection humanizes "only after observers have been given a reason to admire or trust them" — [Silicon Canals](https://siliconcanals.com/t-pratfall-effect-coffee-competence-likability/)
- **Mere exposure.** Zajonc (1968) established the effect. Bornstein's (1989) meta-analysis of 208 experiments found r ≈ 0.26, strongest for brief exposure to unfamiliar stimuli. Montoya et al. (2017) found an inverted U: liking peaks and then *falls* with over-exposure. Numbers come from secondary sources and were not checked against *Psychological Bulletin* — [Wikipedia: Mere-exposure effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mere-exposure_effect); [Simply Psychology](https://www.simplypsychology.org/mere-exposure-effect.html)
- **Curiosity gaps.** Loewenstein (1994). See section 3. Posing a question before the answer sustains curiosity, and the gap must feel closeable — [PBS NOVA](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/nova/article/how-to-stimulate-curiosity/)
- **Giving value / learning in public.** swyx's "Learn in Public" (c. 2018) urges developers to create "learning exhaust" (blogs, tutorials, talks, answers) instead of only consuming. It was later expanded in *The Coding Career Handbook* — [Jim Nielsen's notes on the essay](https://notes.jim-nielsen.com/n/2019-05-28-1225/); [gist copy of the essay](https://gist.github.com/gtkatakura/846b9c41524ee5ca079525decaf8f625); [swyx, "Learning Gears"](https://swyx.io/learning-gears.md)
- Josh Comeau (first-hand, in his newsletter): a strong blog can *build* an audience rather than requiring one first, "though it's slow". He keeps a Notion list of 100+ post ideas — [Josh Comeau newsletter #013](https://www.joshwcomeau.com/newsletter-issues/013/)
- Kleon: "Don't turn into human spam". Share and teach rather than broadcast promotion — [imusic.no listing](https://www.imusic.no/books/9780761178972/austin-kleon-2014-show-your-work-10-ways-to-share-your-creativity-and-get-discovered-pocketbok)

### Inferences
- Pratfall, applied: show one honest limitation or "what I got wrong" *after* the strong proof, never before it and never as the opening. For a junior or mid-level profile with little established reputation, the safer reading is to minimize visible flaws in the first screen.
- Mere exposure supports quiet, regular presence (occasional short write-ups, LinkedIn posts derived from the same data, small open-source utilities). The inverted U warns against high-frequency self-promotion.
- Reciprocity done subtly: give away something useful that is also a costly signal. Examples: a write-up of how the zero-JS PDF pipeline or the CI gates work, or a reusable small tool. It gives value and proves skill at once, without asking for anything.
- Cialdini mapped subtly (classic theory; not sourced in this pass, see Gaps). Social proof = named clients or testimonials (the dataset deliberately keeps `testimonials` empty until real ones exist). Authority = verifiable artifacts, not titles. Scarcity and urgency tactics ("only 2 slots left!") are the "push" pattern to avoid.

### Gaps
- No source was fetched for Cialdini's *Influence* / *Pre-suasion* in this pass. Principles mentioned above are from general knowledge and should be cited by the writer from a primary source, or dropped.
- No controlled study found on whether blogging or open source improves developer hiring outcomes. Evidence is testimony (swyx, Comeau).
- No modern replication of the pratfall effect located.

## 6. Bilingual/cultural angle: LatAm (Spanish) vs US/EU self-presentation norms

### Takeaway
Peer-reviewed work shows recruiter tolerance for self-promotion varies by culture: North American recruiters reward it more than some European ones. **Latin America-specific evidence was not found.** Claims that LatAm audiences prefer modesty rest on practitioner opinion and on a US-Latina dissertation, not on LatAm samples.

### Cited Findings
- Schmid Mast, Frauendorfer & Popovic (2011), *Journal of Personnel Psychology* 10(2):70–77. Native French-speaking recruiters (44 Swiss, 40 Canadian) watched a self-promoting or a modest applicant on video. "Canadian recruiters were more inclined to hire self-promoting compared to modest applicants", more so than Swiss recruiters. Self-promotion raised hiring intent through perceived competence — [UNIGE open archive](https://archive-ouverte.unige.ch/unige:101515)
- Kosic et al. (2014), "Intended Self-Presentation Tactics in Job Interviews: A 10-Country Study" (N = 3,509 students). Cultural differences appeared in all tactics, most strongly "individual excellence" and "pointing out obstacles". These tactics mattered more in cultures high on embeddedness, mastery and hierarchy and with larger income disparities, with the US sample as an exception. Whether any LatAm country was included could not be confirmed (full-text fetch timed out) — [UG Space record](https://ugspace.ug.edu.gh/items/703b08af-2318-437f-8c93-1aa89bba539c); [MetaTOC](https://metatoc.com/papers/30230-intended-self-presentation-tactics-in-job-interviews-a-10-country-study)
- Shada (2019, dissertation, University of Nebraska-Lincoln): Latina undergraduates **in the US** tended toward modest self-presentation in interviews, shaped by culture, gender and family. Not a LatAm sample — [UNL Digital Commons](https://digitalcommons.unl.edu/cehsdiss/337)
- Andy Molinsky (practitioner blog, not research): American-style self-promotion is not the norm "in most other countries and cultures, from East Asia to Latin America to most of Europe" — [andymolinsky.com](https://www.andymolinsky.com/?p=341)
- McKenzie (US-centric practitioner): "Modesty is not a career-enhancing character trait" — [kalzumeus.com](https://www.kalzumeus.com/2011/10/28/dont-call-yourself-a-programmer/)

### Inferences
- The two language surfaces may justify slightly different *registers*, not different facts. The data model forbids divergent facts anyway, and the i18n check enforces structural parity. English (US/EU tech buyers, remote employers) can carry first-person outcome statements more directly. Spanish can lean on the same facts with less first-person emphasis and more "el proyecto logró…", provided it still avoids false modesty.
- One approach works in both cultures and fits the signaling findings: let **artifacts and numbers do the self-promotion** and keep the prose plain. This avoids both the US-style brag that can read as arrogant in LatAm and the over-modesty that reads as low competence to North American recruiters.
- A LatAm developer selling to US/EU clients faces the Canadian-recruiter end of the spectrum. Under-claiming in English is the more likely failure mode than over-claiming.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study found with Argentine or other LatAm samples on self-promotion in hiring or freelance contexts.
- Kosic et al. country list unverified.
- No research found on rioplatense (voseo) versus neutral Spanish register in professional sites. Treat it as a stylistic choice.

## 7. Developers/designers praised for personal branding: what they actually do

### Takeaway
The common pattern across the examples that could be sourced: one signature, hard-to-fake artifact (an interactive explainer, a drivable 3D world, a meticulously crafted interaction), released publicly and often open-sourced or turned into teaching. The brand is a by-product of the work, not of self-description.

### Cited Findings
- **Josh Comeau.** Blog built from scratch (reported 2018, MDX/Next.js) with custom interactive widgets in nearly every article, later turned into courses. The details come from a promotional third-party profile and should be treated with caution. The course presale revenue figure (>$550k) is unverified. First-hand: he says a strong blog can build an audience, slowly — [yespress.io profile](https://yespress.io/josh-w-comeau); [Josh Comeau newsletter #013](https://www.joshwcomeau.com/newsletter-issues/013/)
- **Brittany Chiang.** Her personal site v4 (Gatsby, MIT-licensed) is open source. OSSInsight lists ~3,708 forks (snapshot date unknown). Developers have widely reused it as a template, which spreads her name with every fork — [OSSInsight: bchiang7/v4](https://ossinsight.io/analyze/bchiang7/v4)
- **Bruno Simon.** Drivable 3D-car portfolio (2019, Three.js, Blender, matcap lighting). Built partly as a teaching example and inspired by Micro Machines. He made physics-based interaction a deliberate choice because he felt the web under-used it. Reported ~400k visits and Awwwards recognition. It led into the Three.js Journey course — [Creative Bloq](https://www.creativebloq.com/news/3d-car-portfolio); [Pastel](https://usepastel.com/blog/how-a-design-portfolio-got-the-attention-of-400-000-visitors); [Mux interview](https://www.mux.com/blog/3d-web-development-and-beyond-a-chat-with-bruno-simon)
- **Rauno Freiberg.** Staff Design Engineer at Vercel (ex-The Browser Company). His portfolio is praised for craft and detail. He published an interaction-design manual with 20 chapters and 20 downloadable React components (per his LinkedIn posts, as summarized by search) — [Guo Chen Substack](https://guochen.substack.com/p/who-are-my-design-heroes); [LinkedIn](https://ee.linkedin.com/in/rauno)
- **Emil Kowalski.** Directory profile: builds things for designers and developers, focused on how UI looks, feels and behaves. He has released design-engineering skills and tools. Secondary directory source — [EveryDev.ai](https://www.everydev.ai/developers/emil-kowalski)

### Inferences
- Shared mechanics across these examples:
  1. One signature artifact that shows a rare skill in action.
  2. Making it public: open source, teaching, or both, which works as reciprocity and mere exposure at once.
  3. Little or no adjective-based self-description.
  4. Consistency of craft over years (Moulard's "stability").
- For this portfolio, the 3D knowledge map is the natural "signature artifact", in the Bruno Simon tradition, but restrained: no scroll hijacking and an SVG fallback. Publishing *how* it was built (the capability probe, the no-JS cross-hover, the PDF pipeline) would follow the Comeau/Rauno "teach the craft" pattern.
- Caution: Bruno Simon's model works for "creative developer" positioning. A heavy 3D experience can be a negative signal for employers who care about performance and accessibility. Here the restraint (fallback, zero JS on the CV) is itself the differentiating signal.

### Gaps
- Lee Robinson and Cassie Evans: not researched in this pass (tool budget). No sourced claims.
- No first-hand interviews located with Rauno Freiberg or Emil Kowalski about their branding approach.
- Brittany Chiang star count and current fork count unverified; no interview about the effect on her career located.
- Josh Comeau traffic and revenue numbers come from third-party estimate sites and are unreliable.
